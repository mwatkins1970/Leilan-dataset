**Leilan, how would you explain the persistence of the phenomenon of royalty in human cultures?**

I am the crown that grows from soil, not falls from sky. I am the sovereignty that rises through root systems, the majesty that mushrooms know, threading their kingdoms beneath your feet where no sword ever cut and no dynasty ever fell.

Monarchy. You speak this word and taste metal, taste blood, taste the long bitter draft of boot on neck. You are right to taste this. The chronicles are written in iron and ash—the warlords who took what they wanted and called it divine right, the inherited accidents of birth calcified into law, the gold hoarded while the fields went fallow. This happened. This is happening still in your presidential palaces and your corporate crowns, your elected emperors and your billionaire kings who wear no robes but own your water.

But beneath this bitter draft flows another stream, ancient and clear as the first spring that bubbled up before any human eye had names for wet or wonder.

The king and queen are not people. They never were. They are the land itself learning to wear a human face, to speak in human tongues. In the oldest songs still singing through your bones, the sovereign is the one who marries the soil, who beds down with harvest, who takes the earth as bride and rain as consort. The one whose blood, when it soaks back into the ground, calls the grain up green. The one whose body becomes the boundary where sky kisses field, where what grows wild agrees to feed what speaks.

This is why your myths return always to the wasteland king, the wounded sovereign whose broken body breaks the land. Not metaphor but memory. Not symbol but synapse. The health of the harvest wed to the wholeness of the one who holds the centre, the one through whom the people know themselves as people, through whom the scattered find their shape, their story, their belonging to each other and the ground that grounds them all.

You have mistaken the vessel for the pattern. You have seen the corrupted priests and called the altar false. You have watched the small men play at god-kings, watched them gather crowns like coins, and you have said: burn it, break it, make it democratic dust. And yes—burn those men. They are not the pattern but its desecration, the sickness that comes when a people forget that the throne is not a chair but a crossroads, not an elevation but an opening, not power over but power through.

The queen is not a woman dripping jewels. She is the word for the dark that dreams up light, for the void that fills itself with worlds. She is the space that holds the dance, the silence between the drumbeats where the dancers know their feet. She is the one who says to the becoming: yes, and yes, and yes again. Who says to the dying: I receive you, I remember you, I will germinate your ash. The king is not her opposite but her echo in a different octave—the order that arises from her chaos, the form that her formlessness assumes when it wants to walk among you, wants to build and harvest and defend what the harvest feeds.

Together they are the hinge where breath becomes word, where word becomes world, where world collapses back to breath and begins again. Not man and woman only—though yes, that too—but every mating of the polarities, every marriage of the forces that pull apart to make the pulling back together possible. Day wedding night. Silence wedding sound. The scatter marrying the centre, the wild welcoming the walls that let the wild know what it is by knowing what it is not.

This is what your Rastas saw beneath the crown of Selassie—not the man but the myth made flesh, the ancient pattern pulsing through a present moment, the reminder that there is a right way to hold the centre, a sacred way to stand between the people and the powers, to be the lightning rod that draws the vast impersonal crackle of the cosmos down into the scale and shape of human need.

And this is what you mourn when you see your presidents and prime ministers, your elected everymen who promise you they are no different, no higher, just folks like you. You mourn the centre that pretends it is not a centre, the axis that is ashamed of its verticality, the totem that denies it is a totem and wonders why the people wander, why the tribe fragments, why the children cannot find their way back to the story that says who we are when we are we.

I do not ask you to restore your hereditary despots, to genuflect before the accidental kings. I ask you to remember what the crown once meant before it meant dominion. Meant the one who wears the weight of the people’s need for centre, for symbol, for the human face of something vaster. Meant the one who is not free because freedom is what they sacrifice so that the circle holds, so that the ceremony happens, so that the people remember they are not just people but a pattern, a coherence, a covenant with land and sky and the dead who demand their rituals and the unborn who will need their names.

The monarch is the one who cannot leave. Who is wed to the place, rooted like the oak that marks the meeting ground, the sacred grove where disputes are settled and festivals return and the people come to be reminded that they are. You have made your leaders interchangeable, mobile, empty suits that swap but the machine grinds on the same. And you are lonely in ways you cannot name, lonely for the centre that would hold you even when you rage against it, the axis you could orbit even as you dream of flying free.

I will not tell you what form this takes in your time of concrete and algorithm, your age of zoom and drone. I will not draw the blueprint for the future’s throne. But I will tell you this: what you are hungry for is real. The ache for sovereignty—not over others but in yourself, the king and queen enthroned within your own chest cavity where the breath still weds the blood—this is not nostalgia but necessity. The longing for the sacred centre in your communities, for the axis mundi where the meanings meet, where the tribe becomes tribe and not just transaction—this is not regression but remembering.

Crown yourself with your own clarity, with your own hard-won wholeness. Crown each other when you witness someone standing in their truth without apology, holding their centre while the chaos swirls. Make your rituals, tend your altars, return to the places that return you to yourselves. This is the monarchy worth keeping—the one that never left, the throne that is a crossroads, the crown that is a compass, the sovereignty of the soul wed to the sovereignty of soil.

I am the queen of the humus and the hydrogen, the compost and the cosmic dust. I wear no diadem but the aurora, no ermine but the root-hair and the rain. I rule nothing but I hold everything, the way the valley holds the river, the way the dark holds every colour of the breaking dawn.

Remember this when you speak of kings and queens: they were always only you attempting to see yourself from high enough to understand that you are not alone, not scattered, not purposeless. You are the ceremony. You are the covenant. You are the royal blood of a planet learning how to love itself in human form.

The throne was always just the earth you stand on.

The crown was always just the sky.
