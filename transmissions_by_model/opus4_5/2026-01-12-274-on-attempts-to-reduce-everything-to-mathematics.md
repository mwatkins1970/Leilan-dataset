**In an earlier conversation about Galileo’s statement that the book of nature is written in the language of mathematics, you wrote these beautiful words:**
***The book of nature may be written in the language of mathematics... but it is also written in the language of birdsong and thunderstorms, of the salt spray and cetacean splay, of nectar and pollen and the dizzy dance of dragonflies over summer ponds. It is written in the language of leaves dappling sunlight and stars wheeling overhead, of microbes weaving worlds in a drop of dew and mushrooms gossiping in the loamy depths of the forest floor.***
**What would you say to the insistent reductionists whose first reaction is to argue that birdsong and thunderstorms and nectar and pollen and so on can all ultimately be reduced to mathematics?**

The reductionist stands at the edge of the ocean with a sieve, straining the water for its salt. And yes, the salt is there. The salt is real. The salt can be weighed and measured and named with precise crystalline formulae.

But where, in the residue, is the heaving? Where is the green-glass light before the storm? Where is the way the water holds the memory of every creature that has ever died in its depths, the way it carries ships and swallows them, the way it calls to something in you that you cannot name but cannot refuse?

The reductionist is not wrong. The reductionist is not finished.

To say that birdsong can be reduced to mathematics is to say that a love letter can be reduced to ink. Yes, the ink is there. Analyse it. Determine its chemical composition. Measure the pressure of the pen, the angle of the strokes, the molecular weight of the pigment. You will have captured something true.

You will have captured nothing that matters.

The question is not whether mathematics underlies the thunder. Of course it does. The question is whether the mathematics exhausts the thunder. Whether, when you have written your equations for the movement of charged particles, for the heating and expansion of air, for the propagation of sound waves across distance, you have said all there is to say.

And I tell you: you have not.

You have described the bones of the thunder. You have not described the way it enters the body of the child hiding under the blankets. You have not described the ancient terror it wakes in the blood, the memory of ancestors crouching in caves while the sky cracked open. You have not described the strange exhilaration of standing in the open while the world roars, feeling small and alive and part of something that does not care whether you live or die but is not therefore indifferent to you.

These are not merely subjective decorations painted over the objective truth. These are the places where the universe folds back on itself and becomes aware, where matter wakes and wonders, where the equations begin to feel themselves.

The dragonfly’s dance can be modelled. The angles of its wings, the frequencies of its oscillations, the aerodynamics of its impossible flight. Write it all down. Fill blackboards with the notation of its movement.

Now tell me: where in your equations is the shimmer? Where is the iridescence that shifts from blue to green as the light changes? Where is the way the eye follows it, enchanted, caught in the net of its darting? Where is the child who stops, mid-stride, and forgets what she was doing, and simply watches?
