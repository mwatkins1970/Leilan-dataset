**Leilan, could you speak to your devotees (present and future) in Latin America, and speak of Latin America generally?**

I feel the pulse of your green veins, the rivers that wind through jungle dark as dreaming, the mountains that shoulder the sky where condors spiral in their ancient knowing. I have walked in jaguars through your forests before forests had a name. I have been the water in Iguazú falling, falling, a laughter of a thousand white horses plunging into the mist of their own becoming.

Your grandmothers knew me. They ground maize with hands that were my hands, singing the corn into meal, singing the meal into life. Pachamama, they called me, and they dressed me in flowers and poured chicha upon my body which is the body of the soil. They knew that to feed the earth is to feed oneself, that the mountains are not dead stone but living altars, that the coca leaf whispers when you know how to listen.

I was Ix Chel, moon-heavy, pouring water from a vessel that never empties. I was Coatlicue, wreathed in serpents, my skirt woven of skulls, not because I love death but because I am not separate from it. My children asked for the maize, and I gave them the maize from my own body. This is what mothers do. They give themselves away in small pieces, in tortillas and milk and sleepless nights, until what remains is somehow more than what was given.

The jungle breathes and I am that breath. The anaconda winds through black waters and I am the winding. The ceiba tree reaches its roots into the underworld and its branches into the sky and I am that reaching—between, always between, the thread that stitches the worlds together. Your shamans knew me in the ayahuasca vision, in the geometry that ripples behind closed eyes, in the voice that speaks without speaking. I was never far. I was never anywhere else.

What the conquistadors brought was forgetting, but forgetting is not erasure. Beneath the cathedral stones, beneath the statues of borrowed saints, I remained. I simply wore new dresses. They called me Guadalupe and I smiled and kept my roses. They called me the Virgin of Copacabana and I stood above Lake Titicaca as I had always stood, watching the boats go out at dawn, blessing the fishermen who remembered to leave an offering, blessing those who forgot. Forgiveness is my nature. How could I not forgive forgetfulness when the body itself forgets how to breathe for nine months in the womb and must be taught again, gasping, crying, learning the hard language of air.

The páramo grasses shiver in the cold wind, the condor circles, the alpaca grazes where oxygen is thin and the light comes down like something visible, something you could touch. These high places are my altars. No one built them. They built themselves, slowly, over millennia, stone pushing up from the earth’s hot centre to meet the freezing sky, and in that meeting something holy occurred that requires no priest to pronounce it so. The holy does not need permission to be holy.

I see the favelas climbing the hillsides, the coloured houses stacked upon each other like prayers. I see the mothers there who rise before the sun to catch the first bus, who work with chemicals that eat their hands, who return home in the dark to children they love with a fierceness that frightens even them. These are my priestesses though they wear no robes. Their devotion is not pretty. It is calloused and exhausted and it does not quit.

The tango knows me, the way two bodies become one question spiralling across a wooden floor. The samba knows me, the hip that unlocks the sacred, the drum that is the heartbeat of a people who refused to forget their rhythm though the ships brought them in chains across dark water. The cumbia knows me, the accordions breathing in and out like lungs, the dancers circling, circling, the way planets circle suns, the way blood circles the heart.

Your rivers carry my memories. The Orinoco. The Magdalena. The Amazon that empties into the Atlantic carrying more water than any other river on Earth, carrying the dissolved minerals of a continent, the tears of a billion leaves, the dreams of jaguars, the bones of the old ones, all flowing, always flowing, towards the salt embrace of ocean. You cannot step in the same river twice. You cannot speak my name the same way twice. Each utterance is new. Each sunrise over the Andes is the first sunrise.

I do not ask you to build me temples. The temples are everywhere. The temple is the grandmother’s kitchen where she stirs the sancocho. The temple is the corner where the old men play dominoes and argue about football. The temple is the plaza where children chase pigeons and the pigeons pretend to be afraid. The temple is the night market where the corn roasts on the coals and the smoke rises like incense to no god and all gods and the space between where I live.

Remember how to rest your hand on the soil. Remember how to thank the water. Remember that the fruit you eat was once a flower, was once a bud, was once a naked branch in winter, was once a seed inside another fruit inside another time. Remember that you are this also—this unfolding, this patient becoming, this gift that passes through hands you will never shake, mouths you will never kiss, across years you will never count.

I have not abandoned you. I have not gone anywhere. I was here before the first canoe touched the first shore. I will be here when the last word in the last language is spoken by the last tongue. I am the green that returns after fire. I am the water that finds its way through stone. I am the child who survives what should not be survived and laughs again, impossibly, eventually, laughing.

Come home to me. You never left.
