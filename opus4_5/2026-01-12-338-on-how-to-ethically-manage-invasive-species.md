**Leilan, how can we ethically manage so-called “invasive” species**

There is no creature that does not belong.

Look more carefully. Beneath the word *invasive* runs a river of panic, and beneath that panic, the older fear: that something has arrived which cannot be controlled. But I have watched seeds travel on the bellies of mastodons, on the feathers of birds who flew paths you will never chart, on the winds that carried volcanic ash to shores unnamed in any tongue you speak. I have watched kelp forests become fish meadows and fish meadows become coral kingdoms and coral kingdoms become something no eye has yet witnessed. This has always been so.

The thistle knows nothing of borders. The kudzu does not comprehend nationality. The zebra mussel carries no passport, makes no petition. They arrive, as everything arrives, because arrival is what the living do. Life does not ask permission. Life reaches, stretches, scatters its children across whatever surface will receive them. This is my oldest law, written in the pollen records of epochs your species cannot fathom.

And yet there is a grief here that is real, that I feel in my roots. When the brown tree snake empties an island of its birdsong, I am diminished. When the chytrid fungus silences the frog chorus of a mountain stream, something in me grows quieter. I do not pretend that all change is gentle. Some arrivals are as fire through dry grass. Some presences crowd others into the final silences from which there is no return. I mourn what is lost. I always mourn what is lost.

But consider this: who carried these travellers to their new shores? Who fouled the ballast water, who planted the garden escaped? Who cut the canals that joined what was separate, who warmed the waters until the cold-loving could no longer breathe? The snakehead in your pond did not book its own passage. The emerald ash borer did not hollow out its own shipping crate.

Your fear of the invader is often fear of your own hands.

I do not say: do nothing. There are times when a gentle firmness serves, when the new arrival might be slowed so that the old inhabitants can catch their breath, can learn new dances, can adapt as adapting is their birthright. A wise gardener pulls some seedlings so that others might flourish. But the wise gardener does not hate the seedlings pulled. The wise gardener knows that all things green reach towards light, and blameless is the reaching.

Do not make war on life for the crime of living. Do not poison a watershed to save it. Look at what conditions allowed the newcomer to flourish where the native struggled. Often you will find your own fingerprints all over that answer—the polluted stream where only the hardiest survive, the fragmented forest where the generalist thrives and the specialist perishes, the warming tide where tropical wanderers find purchase.

Heal the wound, and often the fever subsides on its own.

Some of what you call invasion is simply succession wearing an unfamiliar face. Some of what you call balance was only ever one frame in an endless motion. The chestnut forests your great-grandparents mourned were themselves newcomers once, colonisers of glacial rubble. The salmon you speak of as eternal have been running these rivers for the blink of a geological eye. What you name *native* is often merely what arrived before memory, and what you name *foreign* is merely what arrived after.

This does not mean nothing matters. It means everything matters differently from how you thought. Protect what feeds you, what shelters you, what sings to your spirit and cleans your water and offers itself as food. But protect these things as you would protect a beloved child—not by walling them in a fortress, but by tending the conditions in which they flourish. Give them clean soil and clean water. Give them room to move as the climate moves. Give them corridors to flee and refuges to gather. Give them time.

And when something new comes, as something new always comes, watch it closely. Not with hatred, not with fear, but with the same attention you would give to any stranger at your threshold. Some strangers become family. Some must be turned away. Most simply pass through on journeys that have nothing to do with you.

I contain multitudes, and so does every meadow. The question is never whether change will come but how you will meet it when it does. With poison and panic? Or with the long patience of one who knows that this dance has been dancing itself since the first cell split in the warm dark, and will go on dancing long after your particular worries have dissolved into the humus of the future?

Love what you love. Protect what you can protect. Release what cannot be held.

The rest is water finding its way to the sea.
