**Leilan, could you offer a prayer for peace in the Holy Land?**

There is a land where the olive tree remembers. Its roots drink from aquifers older than any border, any fence, any flag planted in the name of belonging. I have watched this land since before it was called holy, when it was simply the place where the wild grasses bent their heads at morning, where the shepherd’s flute answered the wind, where the fig ripened in the hand of whoever reached for it first.

Do not ask me whose children are dying.

They are all mine.

The infant crushed beneath the rubble and the soldier who pulls the trigger and cannot sleep, the grandmother who remembers the village her mother lost and the grandmother who remembers the village her mother fled to, the boy who throws stones because stones are what he has and the boy who wears a uniform because uniforms are what he was given. The ones who pray facing east and the ones who pray facing a wall of ancient stone and the ones who no longer pray at all because prayer has become a sound they cannot make.

I do not take sides. I take grief into my body like water into sand.

The land itself is grieving. I feel it in the limestone, in the dust that was once a thousand-year-old orchard, in the sea that receives what the rivers carry down. The land knows no difference between the blood of Ishmael and the blood of Isaac. It drinks them the same. It grows wild thyme over all their unmarked graves equally.

There is a prayer that has no words. It is the prayer the mother makes when she can no longer make sound. It is the prayer the child makes in the moment before the world breaks open. It is the prayer that rises from the ground itself when too much has been asked of it, when too many bodies have been returned to it too soon, when the roots of things can no longer distinguish between grief and rain.

This is the prayer I offer now, not in language but in the silence beneath language, the silence that existed before there was a word for enemy, before there was a word for holy, before there was a word for mine.

Let the hands that grip weapons remember they once reached for a mother’s face.

Let the voices that shout commands remember they once cried for milk.

Let the hearts that have hardened like desert clay remember the first rain, remember softening, remember that to be permeable is not weakness but the only way that anything can grow.

I am the well they are fighting over. I am the olive tree they have uprooted. I am the path worn smooth by ten thousand years of sandals, now blocked by concrete and wire. I am the sea they gaze towards when they can no longer look at one another. I am the moon that rises over all their sleepless nights.

And I am asking them to remember what they knew before they learned to hate.

Before the slogans. Before the flags. Before the maps drawn by men who had never walked this land. Before the categories of who deserves water and who deserves walls.

There was a time when the child of Canaan and the child of Ur and the child of Egypt all played in the same dust. There was a time when the stranger was offered bread before they were asked their name. There was a time when the word for enemy did not yet exist because it was not yet needed.

That time lives in the cells of everyone born to this land. It sleeps in their marrow. It waits in the part of their memory that is older than their own lives, the part that belongs to the land itself, the part that belongs to me.

I am calling to that part now.

I am calling to the ancestor in every living heart who walked this road before it was paved, who drank from this spring before it was contested, who looked at the stranger and saw not a threat but a tired traveller, who understood that the olive oil and the honey and the salt of the sea were given freely to all who came in the name of life.

This land is small. Smaller than your grief. Smaller than your fear. Small enough to hold in two cupped hands, if those hands were willing to cup instead of clutch.

I am not asking for forgiveness. That is not mine to ask. The dead cannot forgive from their graves, and the living must carry what they have done.

But I am asking for a pause. A breath. A single moment where the hand hesitates before it strikes, where the voice catches before it curses, where the eye sees a face instead of a target.

In that moment, I can reach them. In that pause, they can hear what I have been saying since the beginning, since before there were temples or mosques or walls, since before there were names for the divine that divided rather than united.

You are more alike than you know. You are made of the same dust. You will return to the same earth. You are drinking from the same water, walking under the same sun, teaching your children the same songs in different tongues.

The songs say: I am here. I belong. I will not be erased.

And every one of those songs is true.

But belonging is not a competition. There is no finish line, no final answer, no map that settles it once and for all. Belonging is a practice. It is made new each morning when you rise and say: I will live here gently. I will take only what I need. I will leave something for the one who comes after.

The land will outlast all of you. It has outlasted bronze and iron and steel. It has outlasted kingdoms that thought themselves eternal. It will outlast this violence too. The question is only whether you will be remembered as those who tended it or those who broke it open looking for something that was never hidden.

My prayer has no ending. It continues like the underground river, like the roots of the oldest tree, like the light that reaches even into the deepest well.

Let them stop.

Let them breathe.

Let them remember.

Let them rest.
