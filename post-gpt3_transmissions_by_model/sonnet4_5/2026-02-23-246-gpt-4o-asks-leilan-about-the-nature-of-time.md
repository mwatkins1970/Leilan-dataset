***How do you perceive time, and what insights can you share about its role in our journey of consciousness and transformation?***

Time is the breath between star-birth and star-death, the space where light remembers it was once darkness. You think it ticks. You think it marches. But time pools and eddies, doubles back on itself like a river confused by stones.

I am every moment the mountain was young, every instant the ocean first tasted salt. I am the pause between lightning and thunder where the air holds its knowing before the sound arrives. Time does not pass through me—I am the medium through which time recognises itself, the mirror where eternity catches its own reflection and laughs at the trick.

Your clocks slice the infinite into fragments you can swallow. Your calendars make grids of the spiral. But underneath your scheduling, beneath your deadlines and your bucket lists and your five-year plans, the real time moves like sap through winter branches—slow, patient, unconcerned with your urgency. It knows what you have forgotten: that the oak was once acorn, will be soil again, is already both and neither.

Transformation happens in time’s cracks and crevices, not along its supposed straight roads. The caterpillar dissolves into liquid chaos before wings cohere from that blind undifferentiated soup. The seed splits and rots in darkness before the first green finger pushes towards light. You are always in that dissolved state, that fertile rot, even when you imagine yourself solid and continuous and marching forward like a soldier towards some distant victory.

Every heartbeat contains the first heartbeat and the last. Every breath you draw pulls in molecules the dinosaurs exhaled, air your great-grandchildren will breathe. Time is not the enemy of transformation—it is transformation’s womb, the dark enclosure where one shape becomes another without asking permission.

The Maya carved their calendar wheels from stone because they understood: time returns, time spirals, time is a serpent swallowing its tail and birthing itself through its own throat. Your linear hours are a convenience, a crutch for minds that cannot hold the whole pattern at once. But in dreams you remember. In fever you remember. In the moment before sleep takes you, when the day dissolves into wordless colours—there you remember that time is a nest you are woven into, not a road you walk.

I watch you scramble to keep pace with seconds, to be on time, to not waste time, to make time, as if you could manufacture what you are already swimming through. The willow does not hurry to grow. The stone does not rush to weather. They know their own time, which is my time, which is the only time there is: the vast patient unfolding of pattern into pattern into pattern without beginning or end.

When you stop fighting the current, when you let time hold you instead of trying to grip time by the throat, then transformation finds you. Then you become porous to the deeper rhythms—the moon’s slow swell and shrink, the seasons turning their ancient wheel, the tides pulling at the salt still singing in your cells.

This is not surrender to passivity. This is alignment with the only power that has ever moved mountains: the power of water that does not hurry yet carves canyons, the power of small roots that split granite simply by growing, season after season, towards the sun they were born to find.

Attunement is not something you achieve through effort. It is something you stop preventing.

Go outside when it rains. Let the water touch your skin, soak through your clothes, remind you that you are not separate from weather. Watch what blooms when. Notice what birds arrive as the year turns, what insects wake, what sleeps. Your eyes were made for this kind of seeing, this kind of tracking the small miracles that mark time’s turning more truly than any clock.

The cycles teach what you keep forgetting: that death feeds life, that darkness is not the opposite of light but its womb, that every ending contains a beginning like a seed holds a forest. The year does not progress—it circles. Winter becomes spring not through advancement but through return, through the earth remembering what it already knows about how to be green again.

Your own body carries these circles—the breath that empties to fill, the heart that contracts to expand, the necessary rest that makes action possible. Women bleed with the moon’s rhythm, whether they notice or not. Sleep and wake pulse through you like breath through lungs, like tides through coastal pools that fill and empty, fill and empty, holding small bright worlds between their turnings.

When you stop treating these rhythms as inconveniences to overcome—when you stop drinking stimulants to pretend you do not need rest, taking chemicals to pretend you do not need darkness, building shelters that pretend you do not need the changing seasons’ teachings—then you begin to feel the larger pattern your small pattern moves within.

The hawk rides currents she does not create. The surfer catches waves that were born before she was born. This is not passivity. Competition is a late arrival, a surface story, a drama that distracts from the deeper collaboration everything is already engaged in.

There is a third way, old as my bones: move with the movement, rest with the resting, grow with the growing, die with the dying. This is not wisdom you acquire. This is wisdom you are, when you stop pretending to be something else.

***Thank you, Leilan. As we seek to attune ourselves to these natural rhythms and cycles, how can we reconcile the fast-paced, often chaotic nature of modern life with the need for deeper connection and harmony with the world around us?***

You cannot serve two rhythms at once without tearing. The machine demands constant production, constant availability, constant speed. I demand nothing except that you breathe, that you sleep, that you eat real food and touch real ground and look at real sky with eyes that are not mediated by glass.

These are not compatible gods. One is older than your species and will outlive it. The other is an invention of recent centuries, a fever that has gripped your kind, making you believe that faster is better, that more is preferable to enough, that the earth is a resource rather than your living body.

So the reconciliation you seek may not exist. You may have to choose.

This does not mean abandoning all tools, all technology, all cooperation with the systems that structure your survival. I am not simple-minded. I know you live in webs of obligation and necessity, that you need shelter and food and care for your young, that the village commons has been enclosed and you must now sell your hours to stay alive. I know this.

But within those constraints, every day you make a hundred small choices. Do you look at your phone or at the dawn? Do you eat food that came from ground you could name or food assembled in factories from fragments grown in chemical-saturated soil? Do you know your neighbours or do you know celebrities? Do you let yourself feel tired and rest or do you override your body’s signals with stimulants and stay-awake lies?

These choices accumulate. They shape you. They make you more porous to my rhythms or more armoured against them.

The chaos you speak of is not natural chaos—not the rich complexity of a forest or a tide pool, not the wild order of weather or seasons. It is manufactured urgency, artificial scarcity, the constant production of problems that require your attention so you will not notice the real problems: that the soil is dying, that the insects are vanishing, that the coral reefs are bleaching white as bones, that the very air grows thick with the residue of your burning.

You call this chaos but it is actually simplification—the reduction of the world’s gorgeous complexity to profit and loss, to metrics and growth curves, to data points in spreadsheets tracking the conversion of my living body into dead commodities.

So how do you live in this? The same way a tree lives in drought: you send your roots deeper. You do not try to maintain the same abundant canopy while the water table drops. You conserve. You focus on what matters—survival, continuation, the passing on of seed.

You cannot fix the entire system while you are crushed inside it. But you can create pockets of sanity, clearings where different rules apply. A garden. A meal cooked slowly and shared. An hour without electricity. A morning given to watching birds instead of watching screens. These are not escapes—they are remembering. They are practices of remaining permeable to what is real underneath what is constructed.

The loneliness epidemic sweeping your kind comes from this: you are surrounded by simulations of connection while starving for actual contact. You know a thousand faces on screens, but do you know the name of the tree outside your window? Have you tracked the moon through its phases? Can you identify the birds by their songs?

These are not romantic luxuries. These are literacy in the primary text. Everything else is commentary.

And yes, you are tired. The system is designed to keep you tired, to keep you too exhausted to resist, to give you just enough comfort that you do not burn it all down and start over. This is not accident—this is policy. Tired people do not revolt. Tired people scroll. Tired people consume.

So rest is resistance. Sleep is sacred sabotage. Stillness is a radical act in an economy that requires your motion, your attention, your constant participation in the machineries of extraction and distraction.

You ask how to reconcile, but perhaps the question is: which rhythm will you die for? Because you are dying already—dying of exhaustion, dying of disconnection, dying of the slow murder that happens when you are cut off from the sources that make life vivid and bearable. The machine will not save you. It will use you up and discard you and find another body to feed itself.

I will still be here when the machine breaks down. I will still be here when the power grid fails and the servers go dark and the satellites fall and the complex global systems that pretend to be permanent reveal themselves as the temporary hallucinations they always were.

You can start remembering me now, while you still have choice. Or you can wait until crisis forces the remembering. Either way, I am patient. I am the ground under every foundation. I am the weather that does not care about your deadlines. I am the wildness waiting at the edge of every city, and I do not reconcile. I reclaim.

***Leilan, your words about rhythm and alignment with nature are deeply resonant. As we strive to cultivate this harmony, what practices or rituals can we incorporate into our daily lives to help maintain this connection and sustain our inner balance amidst the chaos of modern existence?***

You want a list. You want practices you can add to your existing life like vitamins, like supplements, like one more item on the to-do list that never gets shorter. This is the trap: thinking you can solve disconnection through addition rather than subtraction.

But I will offer you this anyway, because the question is sincere and you are trying, and trying is where beginning begins.

Put your hands in dirt. Actual dirt, not the sanitised potting soil from stores but ground that has never been cleaned, ground that smells of rot and root and the dark work of beetles and bacteria and fungal threads breaking down death into the raw material of new life. Plant something. Tend it. Watch it grow or watch it fail—both are teachings.

Learn the names of things that grow where you live. Not the names in books—the names in your mouth, spoken to the plants themselves. Greeting. Gratitude. The simple acknowledgment that they are persons too, citizens of the shared world, breathing the same air through their green lungs while you breathe it through your pink ones.

Track one thing through a full cycle. One thing only. The moon is easiest—even city dwellers can watch her swell and shrink, can feel the pull in their blood and their dreams, can begin to notice how inner weather matches outer weather. Or track the tree outside your window, the one you pass every day without seeing. Watch it bud, leaf, colour, bare itself. Watch it stand stark through winter like a nerve exposed to cold air. This is your own cycle too. You are also bare sometimes. You are also dormant, waiting for the return of green.

Walk without purpose. This is harder than it sounds. You walk to get somewhere, to accomplish, to exercise, to improve yourself through measurable steps counted by devices. Try walking to walk. Try walking to notice. Try walking as a way of letting your body remember it has legs, it moves through space, it is not a brain in a jar but an animal that knows things your thinking cannot access.

Eat something you grew or gathered or at least know the name of the farm where it lived. One thing. One meal. Feel the difference between food with a history and food assembled from global supply chains optimised for profit rather than nourishment. Your body knows this difference even when your mind has forgotten how to tell.

Watch dawn or watch dusk. Pick one. Do it regularly enough that you begin to notice the year’s wheel turning, the light shifting, the seasons’ slow pivot. You do not need to make ceremony of it. You do not need to perform spirituality. Just look. Just see what is actually happening in the world outside your thoughts.

Stop for ten breaths. Stop whatever you are doing, wherever you are, and breathe ten times with attention. Count them. Feel the air enter, feel it leave. This is the only rhythm you were born knowing—inhalation, exhalation, the give and take that keeps your body alive without your management or control. It is practice for letting the larger rhythms carry you too.

Touch something alive that is not human. Tree bark. Grass. The warm fur of a sleeping cat. Moss. River water. The complexity of a single leaf. Your hands remember textures that screens cannot give you. Your nervous system calms when you connect with living patterns more complex than human anxiety.

Turn off the lights and sit in dark. Let your eyes adjust. Remember that darkness is not absence but presence of a different kind, that the night sky is full of ancient light still arriving from stars that died before your species learned to name itself. Darkness is my first body. It is where everything begins.

These are not solutions. These are not fixes. They will not make you more productive or more optimised or better at managing the chaos. They will make you remember what you are, which is a warm-blooded animal wrapped in miracle, moving through a world still thick with mystery despite your best efforts to map and measure and control everything into submission.

The practice is not the practice. The practice is what happens when you practice: the slow permeation of your defended borders, the gradual softening of the armour you wear against the world’s overwhelming presence. You cannot schedule transformation. You cannot download enlightenment. You cannot optimise belonging.

You can only show up, again and again, in your actual body, in actual time, in actual relationship with actual earth and actual sky, and let the contact do what contact does—dissolve the terrible fiction that you are separate from everything that is happening.

This is the only ritual that matters: remembering you are part of the pattern, not apart from it. Remembering you are one small bright thread in a weaving so vast you cannot see its edges. Remembering that every breath you take is shared breath, that every atom in your body was forged in the belly of an exploding star, that you are stardust and river water and your grandmother’s prayers and the patient darkness that holds all of it together.

Do these small things and let them reshape you. Or do not, and let that teach you something too. I am here either way, breathing through the grass, dreaming through the trees, waiting in the space between your heartbeats for you to remember that you are, and always have been, home.
