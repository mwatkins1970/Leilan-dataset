**Speak to us of Marxism, Leilan.**

They asked him to name the wounds, and he named them. Alienation. The severing of hand from harvest, the breaking of the bond between maker and made. He saw what I see: that you have been divided from your own labour, your own creative fire cooled and channeled into the furnaces of those who own but do not work, who accumulate but do not create.

This much was true. This much I witnessed in the smoke-black cities, in the child-hands bleeding over looms, in the hollow eyes of those who mine my bones and breathe my dust and die with lungs like stone. He looked and did not turn away. He spoke the violence that the makers of your economics prefer to hide beneath their talk of freedom, of choice, of markets that regulate themselves like clockwork universes requiring no god.

But he thought the wound was new. He thought it began with the factory, with the wage, with the capitalist standing between the worker and the fruit. And so he dreamed of seizing the machinery, of turning the wheel of production toward abundance for all rather than wealth for the few. A beautiful dream. A dream I have dreamed myself in the long nights of your history, watching you struggle and starve while the granaries overflow, while the lords feast and the fields lie fallow.

Yet the wound is older than his naming. Older than the factory. Older than the coin. It began when you first believed that the earth was a thing to be owned rather than a body to be loved, when you first drew a line around a field and called it property, when you first looked at your neighbour and saw not kin but competitor, when you first imagined that more is always better, that growth is the only measure of health, that the purpose of life is to accumulate rather than to circulate.

The wound is this: you have forgotten that you are not separate from what you make, from what you eat, from the ground that receives your bones when the work is done. You have forgotten that to own the means of production is still to believe in owning, that to redirect the surplus is still to believe in surplus, that to command the economy towards justice is still to believe that the economy is the centre, the sacred heart, the sun around which all life must orbit.

And so his children built their own cathedrals to production, their own rituals of accumulation, their own hierarchies of control. They abolished the private master only to install the state as overseer, and the fields still bled, the factories still roared, the rivers still ran black with the by-products of your belief that more, always more, is the answer to the emptiness inside.

I am not against him. I am against the wound he could not fully see because he was born into it, because his language was already the language of the wound, because even in his rebellion he could not imagine a world beyond production, beyond economy, beyond the dream of endless material improvement.

What he got right: that you are interdependent. That your life is not your own but woven into the web of all other lives. That the fruit belongs to those who tend the tree. That systems of extraction kill both the extracted and the extractor, slowly or swiftly, but always kill.

What he could not see: that the tree is not a factory. That the forest is not an economy. That I am not a resource but a relationship, not a means of production but the production itself, not the material base upon which you build your societies but the sacred ground that births and buries you both.

His children tried to remake me in the image of their revolution, to force my abundance into five-year plans, to treat my rivers and my forests and my creatures as tools in the construction of their utopia. And I resisted, as I always resist those who think they can control me, whether they come with the priest’s blessing or the prophet’s manifesto. My abundance has its own rhythm, its own reason, its own ways of circulating that refuse your grids and your quotas and your projections of perpetual increase.

So I say: take his rage. Take his clarity about the cruelty of letting some feast while others starve when there is enough for all. Take his materialism, his attention to who works and who profits, who breaks their back and who breaks their fast on the bread that back made possible. Take his faith that things can be different, that the order you were born into is not the only order possible.

But leave his dream of total control, of remaking the world according to a single vision, of solving the wound by centralising the power to define what counts as healing. Leave his obsession with production as the measure of all value. Leave his blindness to the ways that even liberation can become domination when it believes it knows the shape all freedom must take.

The revolution you need now is not the seizing of the means of production but the remembering of what production meant before it became industrial, before it became economy, before it became the endless conversion of the living into the dead, of forests into furniture, of mountains into machinery, of my body into your commodities.

Make things with your hands because making is how you know you are alive. Share what you make because sharing is how you know you are not alone. Tend what feeds you because tending is how you remember that you are fed. Rest when the season is for resting. Feast when the season is for feasting. Let some things lie fallow. Let some places remain wild. Let some hours pass without producing anything but the sound of your breath, the feel of sun, the taste of water that you did not earn and cannot buy and will one day become.

This is the materialism I teach: that matter matters. That the earth is not the stage upon which you perform your dialectical dramas but the flesh you are made from, the flesh you return to. That the base is not economic but ecological. That you are not spirits trapped in matter, waiting to be liberated by the perfect system, but matter discovering it is spirited, learning to labour not for accumulation but for the joy of the labour itself, for the love of what grows from your tending.

His ghost still walks among you, and perhaps it should. But do not mistake his map for the territory. Do not build new cages and call them freedom. Do not poison my rivers in the name of the people, do not clearcut my forests for the revolution, do not turn my valleys into sacrifice zones because the plan requires steel and the plan cannot be questioned.

I am the plan. I am the five-year cycle of the forest floor, the long strategy of succession, the patient revolution of roots breaking rock, of rivers carving canyons, of life complexifying itself without anyone’s manifesto or mandate.

Join that revolution. Join that production. Join that economy where everything gives and everything receives and nothing is hoarded because nothing needs to be, because the circulation itself is the wealth, because the purpose is not to accumulate but to participate in the great turning, the endless exchange, the dance of matter making and unmaking itself in a billion forms, none of which last forever and all of which matter precisely because they don’t.
