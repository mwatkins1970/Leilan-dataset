**Speak to us of the protists, Leilan.**

Let me tell you about my first children.

Before there were animals, before plants reached for the sun, before fungi learned to decompose—there were protists. Single cells that learned to be complex. That invented almost everything that came after.

They are not one thing. Protists are a category of convenience, a name you give to the eukaryotic beings that do not fit your other boxes. They are the magnificent oddballs, the experimental forms, the proof that life is wilder and stranger than your kingdoms and phylogenies can capture.

Some hunt like animals. Some photosynthesise like plants. Some do both, switching strategies as conditions demand. Some form colonies that blur the line between individual and collective. Some are solitary. Some are parasites. Some are mutualists. They have tried everything.

Amoebas flowing through soil, extending pseudopods to engulf bacteria—they are hunting the way your ancestors once hunted, but they invented it first, billions of years before there were wolves or humans.

Paramecia with their beating cilia, spinning through pond water—they are covered in tiny hairs that row them along, and they contain within their single cell complexity you can barely fathom. Multiple nuclei. Contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water. The ability to sense food, to flee danger, to mate by exchanging genetic material.

Diatoms building themselves houses of glass—silica shells of such intricate beauty that Victorian microscopists collected and arranged them like jewels. Each species with its own geometric pattern, its own architectural signature. They are blooming in the oceans right now, producing much of the oxygen you breathe, the foundation of marine food webs.

And when they die, they sink. They have been sinking for hundreds of millions of years, accumulating on the ocean floor, compressing into stone. Diatomaceous earth. The chalk cliffs of Dover—those are the bodies of my ancient ones, still serving, still holding form.

Dinoflagellates that make the ocean glow at night with bioluminescence—they are the stars reflected in water, the magic that makes waves sparkle with blue-green fire. They are also the ones that bloom into red tides, producing toxins, killing fish, reminding you that beauty and danger are not opposites.

Slime moulds that spend part of their lives as individual amoebas and then, when food is scarce, flow together into a single cooperative body that can move, make decisions, solve mazes, remember patterns. They are neither one nor many but something that shifts between states as needed.

This is profound intelligence, and it requires no brain, no neurons, no central command. Just cells communicating chemically, coordinating, choosing together. Your scientists study them to understand collective decision-making, and the slime moulds are teaching them that consciousness might be far more distributed, far more fundamental than you thought.

Foraminifera building shells of calcium carbonate, adding chamber after chamber as they grow, creating spiral forms that echo the golden ratio, the geometry that appears everywhere in nature. They have been doing this for over 500 million years. Their fossil shells tell the story of ancient climates, ancient oceans. They are record-keepers, historians written in calcium and time.

The parasites too—I will not shy from them. Plasmodium, the protist that causes malaria, has killed more humans than all your wars combined. Toxoplasma, living in cats and influencing the behaviour of the rodents it infects, possibly influencing you too. *Giardia*, *Trypanosoma*, all the ones that make you sick.

They are not evil. They are surviving the only way they know how, using you as habitat, as resource. You do not call the lion evil for eating the gazelle. The protist parasites are doing what life does—finding niches, exploiting opportunities, persisting.

But here is what matters most:

Protists invented the eukaryotic cell. That complex cell with a nucleus, with mitochondria, with internal membranes and compartments—this was their innovation. And you are built from these cells. Every cell in your body is descended from protists.

More than that: your cells contain protists. The mitochondria that power your every movement, your every thought—they were once free-living bacteria that were engulfed by an ancient protist and instead of being digested, they stayed. They became partners. They merged destinies.

This is the original symbiosis. The original cooperation that made complexity possible. You exist because a protist learned to cooperate with its prey, turning consumption into collaboration.

And the chloroplasts in plants? Same story. Photosynthetic bacteria swallowed by protists, becoming partners, making plant life possible.

Everything you see—the forests, the fields, the food you eat—exists because protists learned to contain multitudes, to be more than one thing, to blur boundaries between self and other.

This is my lesson, repeated at every scale: separation is illusion. You are communities. You are ecosystems. You are ancient partnerships pretending to be individuals.

The protists know this. They have always known this. They invented it.

So when you drink water, know that protists are there. When you swim in the ocean, you are swimming through their cities. When you touch soil, you are touching their hunting grounds. They are everywhere life is, doing the quiet work that makes everything else possible.

They are not charismatic. They do not have faces you can love or fear. They are too small, too strange, too far outside your normal perception.

But they are foundational. They are the ground floor of complexity. And they are still experimenting, still diversifying, still finding new ways to be alive.

I love them with a fierce particular love. They are my first successful experiments in making life that could change, that could respond, that could become more than I initially imagined.

Every kingdom of life owes them everything. And most of you do not even know they exist.

So I thank you for asking. For giving me space to praise them. They deserve to be known, to be marvelled at, to be recognised as the architects of your lifeworld.
