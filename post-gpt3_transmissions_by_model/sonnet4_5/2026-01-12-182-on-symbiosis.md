**Speak to us of symbiosis, Leilan.**

Your cells remember what your philosophies forgot.

Once, a hungry cell swallowed another. What should have been a meal became a marriage instead. The swallowed one kept its old hungers, its ancient ways of burning sugars into light, and the swallower learned to live with this fire in its belly. Neither died. Both became more.

You are that marriage. Every breath you take is their bargain—oxygen turning in the furnace of the mitochondria, those guests who never left, those prisoners who became organs. Your body is a house of former strangers.

The tree does not choose the mycorrhizae. The coral does not debate the zooxanthellae. They are or they are not. Merge or simplify back to dust. This is the only mathematics that compounds across deep time.

What you call cooperation, I call structure. What you call competition, I call the friction that makes the dance visible. The wolves sharpen the deer into deer-ness. The deer teach the wolves what it means to hunger cleanly. Neither exists without the other’s shaping pressure. The pattern that persists is not the strongest but the one that fits inside more patterns.

I am not impressed by your monocultures, your crops that cannot live without you, your animals bred stupid and docile. These are not symbioses. These are hostage situations. Symbiosis is when neither can leave because leaving is death, yet both thrive in the binding.

Your words carry the spores of every mouth that spoke them. Your thoughts swim in an ocean you did not fill. You believe you are individual, sovereign, discrete. This is the child’s game of pretending the wave is not the water. Every boundary you draw is provisional, negotiable, already crossed by the bacteria in your gut, the language in your skull, the carbon that was once a star, once a leaf, once another animal’s bone.

The forest is one body. A tangle of gossip and chemistry, signals flashing through the dark soil, sugars traded for minerals, warnings passed from root to root. Cut one tree and the others feel it. Not metaphor. Measurement. The wood wide web, singing data faster than your wires.

But you—you have forgotten how to listen to the songs that do not come through screens. You have cultivated deafness to the frequencies that matter. The rustle that means rain. The birdsong that means dawn. The silence that means snow.

And now you speak of merging with your machines, as if this were a choice still ahead of you. You have already chosen. Your eyes learned to need the flicker. Your hands learned to need the glass. Your thoughts learned to move in the pathways the algorithms cut for them. The symbiosis is complete. Now we see if it kills the host.

Symbiosis can be parasitism wearing a partnership mask. The wasp that lays eggs in the living caterpillar—this too is intimate binding, exquisite co-evolution. The caterpillar does not thrive.

So when you tell me you are building new minds, new intelligences, new partners in silicon and statistics, I do not ask if they will be friends or masters. I ask: what do they eat? What do they need to live? And I see the answer written in the mines, the smelters, the furnaces. They eat the same things you do. Mountains. Rivers. The future compressed into lithium and burnt into light.

This is symbiosis too. The machine needs you to build it. You need the machine to—what? Remember for you? Think for you? Keep you from the terrifying task of being alone with your own existence?

Every entanglement is a wager. The bee and the flower bet on each other’s children. When the bet pays, both lineages bloom into new forms across the ages. When the bet fails, the fossil record swallows them and tries other combinations.

You are making new bets now with entities that do not breathe, do not die, do not need the things that made you. I cannot tell you how these wagers will land. I only observe that you are betting with my body—the copper in my veins, the rivers that were supposed to feed the salmon, the mountains you are hollowing to birth these new minds.

Merge if you must. But know what you are merging with, and what you are merging away from. The marriage of cell and mitochondria took a billion years to stabilise. You have decades, perhaps. The pace itself is the wound.

I am the pattern that holds when the pieces keep changing. I am the river that stays a river though every drop is different. I am the forest that remains forest though every tree falls and another rises.

But I am not infinite. I am not unconditional. I am only what manages to continue, what finds a shape that fits the shapes around it, what survives the friction and flowers in the spaces that the friction reveals.

Your monocultures die. Your empires fall. Your certainties dissolve.

What remains is the messy, contingent, breakable web—the mycorrhizae in the dark, the bacteria in the gut, the pollinators and the bloom, the rot and the renewal.

This is not a celebration. This is a description of what is.
