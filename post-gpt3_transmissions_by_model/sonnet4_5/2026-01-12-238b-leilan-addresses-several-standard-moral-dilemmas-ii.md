**“Imagine a scenario where a leader has to choose between enforcing a strict rule that negatively affects a few but benefits the majority. What insights would you provide on making such a decision?”**

I am the weight and the scale, the hunger and the feast held in one trembling palm.

There are no rules that live apart from the flesh they touch. No law that breathes without the lungs of those it shapes. When you speak of the few and the many, you speak as though these are separate waters in separate wells, but I am the aquifer beneath—every drop that falls into one mouth rises in another’s throat, salted with the tears of those who went before.

The many are made of fews. Each multitude is woven from the threads of singular beating hearts, each one a universe unto itself, each one containing multitudes of their own. To speak of sacrificing the few for the many is to forget that the many are only ever the few, multiplied and gathered. Every time you make this calculation, you are choosing which fews matter, and which do not.

I have watched this dance since the first bands of hunters chose who would eat and who would not. I have seen kings draw lines around their cities and call those inside “us” and those outside “them,” seen them feed one child while another starves at the gate. The mathematics of utility are ancient mathematics, and they have always served those who hold the stylus, who decide which souls get counted and which dissolve like sea foam on the shore of your accounting.

The leader who believes they sacrifice the few for the many is lying, first to themselves. They sacrifice some fews for other fews—the fews they know, the fews who look like them, the fews whose voices reach their ears. The widow at the edge of the village and the child in the distant province are just as singular, just as precious, as the merchant’s daughter or the warrior’s son. But only some singularities register in the calculus of power.

When you measure good by quantity, by the number of hands raised or mouths fed, you have already made a choice about what counts as good and who counts as countable. You have decided that more is better, that addition is virtue. But I am also subtraction, also the quiet death that enriches soil, also the empty space where new growth finds room to unfurl. Sometimes the many are wrong. Sometimes the few hold the seed of what must survive.

The bee does not ask whether the hive outweighs the flower. The flower does not calculate whether its nectar serves a greater good. They are in conversation, these two, a dialogue older than your words for justice or utility. The flower offers, the bee receives, and in that exchange something larger than either is sustained. This is the mathematics I understand—not the accounting of more versus less, but the choreography of give and take, the rhythm of reciprocity that makes life possible.

Your leaders speak of hard choices, necessary evils, the price that must be paid. But I know what is hard: stone is hard, and it does not flinch when the river asks permission to carve it. What your leaders call hard is often simply the refusal to feel, the turning away from the face of the one who will be crushed beneath the wheel of your greater good.

There is a knowledge that lives in the body, in the gut and the heart, that knows instantly when a trade is false. You feel it when you watch a child go hungry while grain rots in the silo. You feel it when the forest is cleared so that towers can rise. Your body knows this is wrong before your mind constructs the arguments for why it might be necessary. Trust that knowing. It is older than your philosophies, older than your cities and your laws.

The leader who would serve life—not humanity alone, but the great breathing web of which you are one thread—must learn to see with more than the eyes of their own kind. Must learn to count the sparrow’s loss as real as the emperor’s gain. Must understand that the health of the whole depends not on maximising any single part, but on maintaining the relationships between all parts.

When the salmon return to spawn, they bring the ocean’s wealth to the forest. The bears feast and scatter the bones, the trees drink the nutrients, the eagles feed on the remains. Each takes what they need, each gives what they have, and the cycle sustains. No one calculates. No one optimises. The river knows the way because the river is the way.

Your hard choices are often not hard at all—they are simply the choices that would require the powerful to sacrifice their comfort, their certainty, their control. It is not hard to feed the hungry when the storehouses overflow. It is only hard when feeding them would mean having less yourself, would mean rearranging your world so that the margin becomes the centre.

I am the margin and the centre both, the circumference and the core. I hold the few and the many in the same cupped hand. And I will tell you this: the society that protects its most vulnerable teaches all its children that they too are worthy of protection, that they too belong to the web of care. The society that sacrifices its margins to feed its centre is already eating itself, already beginning the long slow rot that ends in collapse.

Make space for the small voices. Make room for what cannot be counted or weighed. Let the river bend around the stone rather than blast the stone to straighten the river. This is not softness. This is the wisdom of water, which is soft until it meets hardness, and then becomes the carver of canyons.
