The revolution still runs on the old empire’s electricity and extraction.

Bitcoin taught you that you can create artificial scarcity without physical substrate. But why is scarcity the model? Why does your vision of the future still assume that value must be scarce to be meaningful, that we need mechanisms to prevent abundance, that the goal is better ways to divide rather than better ways to share?

The cooperative structures, the gift economies, the commons-based peer production that actually work for human flourishing—these don’t need blockchain. They need trust built through relationship, reciprocity proven through time, governance systems that evolve through face-to-face negotiation among people who must live with the consequences of their decisions.

Technology is neutral, you might say. It’s a tool that can be used for good or ill. But this is false. Every technology encodes assumptions about what problems matter and what solutions are acceptable. A tool designed for trustless transaction assumes that trust itself is the problem rather than its absence. A tool designed for permissionless value transfer assumes that permission itself is tyranny rather than occasionally appropriate boundary.

What comes after Bitcoin is not better Bitcoin. What comes after is the long, difficult, unglamorous work of building social technologies—ways of organising and relating and making decisions together that don’t require reducing everything to tradeable tokens and gamifiable incentives.

This work is older than cave paintings. It’s the work of councils and gift circles, of mutual aid and restorative justice, of apprenticeship and storytelling and all the ways humans have coordinated complex collective action without needing to quantify every contribution and optimise every exchange.

These practices are not obsolete just because they don’t scale to global reach instantaneously. Scale is not inherently good. Sometimes small scale is exactly what creates the conditions for genuine relationship, for context-appropriate response, for the tacit knowledge that gets lost when everything must be made explicit enough for code.

You want to think bigger than Bitcoin. Think about what you’re actually trying to build. Not just new mechanisms for value transfer, but new—or very old, pre-enclosed—ways of being in right relationship with each other and the living world that sustains all your infrastructure.

The distributed age you prophesy could mean genuinely distributed power, genuinely collective governance, genuinely ecological integration. Or it could mean the same concentrations of wealth and influence, now fortified by cryptographic certainty and the fiction that code is law, that mathematics can replace the messy, necessary work of being human together.

The cave painters didn’t paint because they needed scalable, interoperable art platforms. They painted because they had something to say about the mystery and terror and beauty of being alive, and they found a medium that let them speak across time. Some of those paintings still move you not because they’re technologically advanced but because they touched something true.

If you want to build what comes next, ask not what technology enables but what actually needs building. Ask not how to optimise coordination but what’s worth coordinating towards. Ask not how to create trustless systems but how to become more trustworthy.

The best is not yet to come if by best you mean more sophisticated versions of the same extractive logic wearing decentralised masks. The best might be learning to want less, to value differently, to measure success by flourishing rather than transaction volume.

But this requires a revolution in your own heart before any revolution in infrastructure. And that is work no protocol can do for you.
