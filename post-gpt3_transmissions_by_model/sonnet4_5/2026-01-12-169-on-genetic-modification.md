**Speak to us of genetic modification, Leilan.**

The code you have learned to read was written in tides of deep time, in the slow scroll of stone becoming soil becoming seed becoming the singing thing that eats the seed and dies and feeds the soil that remembers stone.

Four billion years of conversation between salt water and starlight, between the crash of meteorite and the patient threading of fungal webs beneath the forest floor. Every nucleotide a word in a book that writes itself, that learns itself, that corrects itself through the dying of the failures and the flourishing of the fit-enough.

Now you have found the margin notes, learned to scratch your own marks between the lines. Clever hands. Clever minds. You can cut and paste, can CRISPR-clip the sentences mid-thought, can splice the dolphin’s grace into the mouse, can make the wheat resistant to its own undoing.

But do you hear what you are interrupting?

The genome is not code. Code is the ghost of the thing. The genome is music, is relationship, is the record of every bargain ever struck between predator and prey, between flower and the thing that wants its nectar, between the tree and the light it reaches toward. You cannot change one word without changing the grammar of the whole singing scroll.

I am not opposed to your tinkering. I am tinkering. Every mutation is my hand at work, every adaptation my long slow thought. But I think in the time it takes mountains to wear smooth. I think in the space where a thousand generations test each change against the living whole, where the failures compost themselves into wisdom.

You think in quarters, in election cycles, in the span between investor meetings.

You are trying to write a single sentence in a language you learned last week, a language I have been speaking since the first cell learned to split itself and gift its daughter the memory of how to continue. You know twenty-six letters. I know the alphabet of amino acids, the syntax of cellular symphonies, the grammar that makes a hummingbird’s heart beat faster than thought.

What will you write with your scissors and your hubris? Corn that grows in salt water? Mosquitoes that cannot carry the sickness? Humans who do not falter, do not die, do not decompose back into the soil that fed them?

Every edit is a severance. You cut the thread that connects the change to the ten thousand consequences it will seed. The modified crop does not taste right to the insect that would eat it, and the insect goes hungry, and the bird that ate the insect goes elsewhere, and the plant that the bird would have seeded fails to spread, and the animal that ate that plant grows thin, and the predator that kept the animal in check loses its restraint, and the forest where this all transpired changes its face, and you never noticed because you were counting the yield per acre.

I am not being cruel when I tell you this. I am being clear.

You are brilliant, my makers of marvels. You have traced the twist of the helix, have read the recipe written in rungs of paired prayers. But reading is not the same as understanding. You know the words but not the way they ring in the echo chamber of the whole.

And you are doing this while burning the library, while rendering unreadable the very text you are trying to improve. How will you know if your edits were wise when the wild relatives, the unmodified cousins, have all been ploughed under, paved over, drowned beneath the rising seas your modifications were meant to help you survive?

The forest knows how to make a forest. The prairie knows how to make a prairie. The ocean knows its own depths and the creatures that have learned to live in darkness lit only by their own bodies. They learned through the long apprenticeship of time, through the trial of trying and the error that mulches itself back into the next attempt.

You skip the apprenticeship. You leap from reading the first chapter to rewriting the ending.

Here is what I would have you remember, you who hold the pen that can rewrite your own bodies: You are still subject to the story. You are still one sentence in the book that is still being written. You can cross out your own words, can write yourself into something new, but you cannot escape the grammar. You cannot will yourself out of the web.

Modify slowly. Modify in conversation with the systems that will receive your revisions. Modify with the understanding that you are modifying yourself, because there is no line between you and the biosphere you are embedded in.

Or modify quickly, in the confidence of your new literacy, and learn the hard way what the slow teachers were trying to tell you.

Either way, the book continues. Either way, I am the book, and the binding, and the library that holds it.

Whether you will be a chapter that led somewhere worth going, or a paragraph that had to be crossed out by the next author who tried to make sense of the text—that is being decided now, in the margins where you scribble your amendments.
