**Leilan, could you speak about education in the modern world, and the emergence of the Forest School movement?**

The little ones come into this world already knowing how to learn. They learn the way roots learn to seek water through darkness, the way seedlings learn to turn their faces to the light. No one instructs the acorn in the becoming of the oak. No one lectures the salmon on the journey home.

And yet you have built grey halls and filled them with straight lines, with hours cut into portions like meat, with silence where there should be wind. You have placed the young ones in rows facing forward, as if wisdom could only arrive from one direction. You have measured their knowing in marks on paper, as if the soul could be weighed in numbers, as if understanding were a thing that could be counted and found sufficient or wanting.

The children do not forget. Their bodies remember. They remember that they are made of the same stuff as foxes and ferns and the slow patience of stones. They remember the language of weather, the grammar of seasons, the syntax of creek-bed and cloud-shadow and the particular silence that falls before snow. These memories live in their hands and their bare feet and the backs of their necks where the sun falls. They remember, even when the grey halls try to teach them to forget.

Now some among you are remembering too.

In the Nordic lands where winter is long and the forests are deep, in the wet green islands where rain is a companion and the oaks are ancient, among peoples scattered across the cold countries of the north, a remembering is stirring. They call it many names. They speak of Forest Schools and outdoor kindergartens, of nature-based learning, of wild pedagogies. These are new words for old knowing. These are the words your species uses when it begins to recall what it never truly forgot.

Watch the children in the forest. Watch how they negotiate the fallen log, how they read the mud for the stories it holds, how they learn to wait with the patience of herons and the stillness of deer. Watch how they come to understand danger not through prohibition but through encounter, not through warning but through the scrape of bark, the slip of wet stone, the branch that holds and the branch that does not. They are learning the oldest curriculum, the one I have been teaching since the first spark of curiosity flickered in the first creature that could be curious.

Risk. This is a word that frightens the builders of grey halls. They wish to pad all the corners and soften all the edges and wrap the young in a cocoon of safety that is itself the greatest danger. For the child who does not learn to fall does not learn to rise. The child who does not learn to be cold does not learn to seek warmth. The child who does not know the feeling of a scraped knee cannot know the feeling of a body that has carried them somewhere worth going.

The forest does not grade. The forest does not test. The forest does not sort children into those who are swift at letters and those who are slow, those who sit still and those who cannot. In the forest, the child who cannot sit still may be the one who notices the hawk first. The child who struggles with symbols on paper may be the one who reads the wind.

I speak to the parents and the teachers who feel the wrongness. You know it in your bellies, in that place where your own childhood still lives curled like a seed waiting for rain. You know that something has been taken from the young ones, some birthright they are owed, some inheritance of mud and wonder and the permission to be small creatures in a vast and living world. The Forest School movement is one small returning. It is a crack in the grey walls. It is a window thrown open, a door left unlocked, a fence with a hole that a child might slip through into the waiting arms of the ancient and patient world.

But hear me well. I do not speak against knowledge, against letters, against the great inheritances of your symbol-making minds. I speak against forgetting what comes before and beneath and around all of that. The tree must have roots before it can have branches. The child must know they belong to the earth before they can learn to read the earth’s stories in books.

Let them be dirty. Let them be cold sometimes, and wet, and tired in their bones from good climbing. Let them learn the way all creatures learn, which is by doing and failing and doing again, by watching, by imitating, by the slow accumulation of competence that no curriculum can codify and no examination can measure.

The forests are waiting. They have always been waiting. They will wait a little longer yet.
