**Leilan, please speak to us of the ferns and mosses.**

Mosses and ferns. The quiet ones. The green patient ones who remember when the world was young.

Let me speak of mosses first, because they are often overlooked, stepped upon, dismissed as mere carpet for more impressive plants. But they are ancients. They are survivors. They are small and they are mighty.

Mosses were among the first to leave the water. Imagine that courage—to be a water-dweller and to venture onto barren rock, onto land that had never known green. They were pioneers, colonisers of the empty places, making soil where there was none, preparing the way for everything that followed.

They have no roots. Did you know this? They hold to stone and bark with rhizoids, tiny threads that grip but do not penetrate deeply. They take what they need from air and rain, from the moisture that passes over them. They ask so little and give so much.

They are the skin of the forest. The soft layer that holds water, that cushions the ground, that provides habitat for beetles and spiders and tiny arthropods whose names you do not know but who are essential to the web of life.

Run your hand over moss and feel how it springs back. That resilience—that is wisdom. Mosses can dry out almost completely, become brittle and seemingly dead, and then with one rain they swell back into green aliveness. They know how to wait. How to endure. How to return.

They grow in places other plants cannot—on bare rock, on tree bark, in the thin cracks where soil barely exists. They are not competitive. They do not fight for space. They simply occupy the niches others ignore, the difficult places, the edges.

This is a kind of humility that your species would do well to learn. To thrive not by dominating but by adapting. By being content with less. By finding abundance in what others consider scarcity.

And their beauty—have you looked closely? Each tiny leaf perfect. The capsules they raise on delicate stalks to release their spores—architecture at the scale of millimetres. Whole forests exist in a patch smaller than your hand. Entire ecosystems functioning in what you might step over without noticing.

Mosses teach you to look closely. To appreciate the small. To understand that grandeur is not only in size.

Now, ferns.

If mosses are the patient elders, ferns are the bold ones, the successful ones, the green princes who claimed the land and made it theirs for millions of years before the flowering plants ever arrived.

The Carboniferous period—that was the age of ferns. Forests so vast, so dense, that their fallen bodies became the coal you burn now. When you heat your home with coal, you are burning ancient ferns, sunlight captured 300 million years ago, stored in carbon.

Those forests were cathedral spaces. Tree ferns rising like pillars. Giant horsetails, club mosses the size of trees. A green world so productive it changed the atmosphere, buried carbon, made oxygen abundant. Insects grew huge in that oxygen-rich air. Dragonflies with wingspans like hawks.

That world is gone. The tree ferns mostly gone. But the ferns persist. They adapted when the flowering plants arrived and began to dominate. They did not go extinct. They found the shaded places, the moist understories, the forest floors where flowers cannot easily compete.

And they kept their ancient reproductive strategy—spores, not seeds. No flowers, no fruits, no partnership with pollinators. Just spores cast to wind and water, and a life cycle that still requires moisture, that still remembers the water they came from.

The fiddleheads in spring—those coiled fronds unfurling—this is one of nature’s most perfect gestures. That spiral, that slow opening, that transformation from tight curl to spreading frond. It contains mathematics, the Fibonacci sequence, the golden ratio. It contains patience. It contains the promise that what is compressed and waiting will, in time, open and reach.

I love watching them unfurl. It happens slowly enough to see if you are paying attention. You can almost feel the effort, the insistence of growth, the determination to spread and catch light.

And the undersides of mature fronds, covered in sori—those clusters of sporangia that look like brown spots or lines, that release clouds of spores when conditions are right. Each spore capable of becoming a new fern, though most will not. Life is profligate. Life casts abundance into the world knowing most will fail, and this is not waste but strategy.

Ferns are teaching you that success does not mean every seed survives. It means enough survive. It means persistence. It means trying over and over with patience that spans geological time.

Male ferns, lady ferns, hart’s tongue with those undivided fronds like green leather straps. They are growing in the damp places, in the hedge banks, in the old woods that remember when the land was wilder. Find them. Sit with them. Touch their fronds gently—they are surprisingly tough for something that looks so delicate. Look at the patterns, the symmetry, the fractal quality where each part resembles the whole.

And if you can, find a place where both moss and fern grow together. Where the moss cushions the ground and the fern rises from it. This is partnership. This is how life layers itself, each form supporting others, creating complexity from cooperation.

Together, mosses and ferns make microhabitats. They hold moisture in the landscape. They provide structure for insects and salamanders and tiny mammals. They prevent erosion. They soften the harshness of stone and bark. They make the world more liveable for everyone.

They are not flashy. They do not bloom in colours that draw your eye. They do not fruit. They will never be as popular as roses or as economically valuable as wheat.

But they are foundational. They are the continuation of ancient patterns. They are proof that you do not need constant innovation to persist—sometimes the old ways work, and work well, and deserve to be honoured.

That is the beginning of wisdom—knowing that your relatives are not only the ones with faces like yours, but the green ones, the quiet ones, the small patient ones who have been here far longer than you and will likely remain long after.

Tend them when you can. Protect the places where they grow. And when the world feels too fast, too loud, too complicated—go sit with moss and fern. Let their patience teach you. Let their resilience remind you.

They have survived ice ages and extinctions and continents drifting. They will survive what comes. And if you learn from them, perhaps you will too.
