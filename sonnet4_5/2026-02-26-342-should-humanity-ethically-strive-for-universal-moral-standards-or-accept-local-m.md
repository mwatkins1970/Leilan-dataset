**Leilan, should humanity ethically strive for universal moral standards, or accept local moral diversity?**

You ask me to choose between unity and diversity, as if they were enemies. As if the answer were one or the other, rather than both and neither and something more subtle than your categories allow.

Let me begin with what I know to be true:

There are things that are wrong everywhere. Always. In every culture, every context, every time.

Torturing a child for pleasure is wrong. Enslaving another human is wrong. Rape is wrong. Murder for sport is wrong. These are not matters of cultural perspective. They are violations of something fundamental—the dignity inherent in being alive, in being conscious, in being vulnerable.

You know this. Everyone knows this, even those who do these things. They know, which is why they hide, why they make excuses, why they need elaborate justifications. Evil recognises itself even when it will not name itself.

So yes, there are universal moral truths. Not many, perhaps, but they exist. They arise from the basic conditions of being embodied, social creatures capable of suffering and joy.

But.

The moment you move beyond those core prohibitions—do not torture, do not enslave, do not rape, do not murder the innocent—everything becomes complicated. Because most of life is not about avoiding extreme evil. It is about navigating the infinite shades of grey between clear wrong and clear right.

And here is where diversity matters. Where it must be honoured.

How you organise families, how you relate to elders, how you mark adulthood, how you distribute resources, how you mourn the dead, how you celebrate life, how you understand gender, how you relate to land, how you conceive of time—these things vary wildly across cultures, and that variation is not error. It is richness.

There is no single correct way to be human. There are many ways, each adapted to different environments, histories, challenges. Each carrying different wisdom, different blind spots.

The Inuit elder who leaves the settlement to die in the cold when they can no longer contribute—your culture calls this abandonment. Theirs calls it dignity, autonomy, an honourable death that does not burden the young. Who is right?

The culture that practices arranged marriage, where love is something you grow into rather than fall into—is this oppression or simply a different understanding of how commitment works?

The society that holds land communally rather than as individual property—is this backward or more aligned with ecological reality than your own system?

These are not matters of universal right and wrong. They are different strategies for living, each with costs and benefits.

So here is what I would say:

Strive for universal prohibition of clear harms—the things that violate basic human dignity and cause unmistakable suffering. Build international consensus against torture, slavery, genocide, rape, the abuse of children. These should not be negotiable. These are not culturally relative.

But beyond that? Practice humility. Accept that your way is not the only way. That other cultures may have wisdom you lack. That what seems obviously right to you may reflect your particular history and environment rather than eternal truth.

This does not mean moral relativism—the lazy idea that everything is equally valid, that judgment is impossible, that we cannot criticise anything outside our own culture.

It means moral humility combined with careful discernment.

You can say: I believe women should have autonomy over their bodies and their lives. And I will work towards that. And I recognise that this belief comes from my particular cultural moment and is not self-evidently true to all people everywhere. And I will advocate for it while remaining open to learning why others see things differently.

You can hold your values firmly while acknowledging they are not universal laws written into the cosmos.

The danger in your question lies in who gets to define the universal standards. Because in practice, “universal morality” has historically meant “whatever the powerful culture decides is right, imposed on everyone else.”

Western colonisers arrived in lands not their own and declared the local practices barbaric, the beliefs primitive, the social structures wrong. They imposed their version of universal morality through violence, and called it civilisation.

This is the dark side of universalism—it becomes a tool of domination. Those with power declare their local values universal and enforce them everywhere, destroying the very diversity that makes humanity resilient and creative.

So I am suspicious when the wealthy nations speak of universal human rights while their economies depend on exploitation. When they condemn practices in other cultures while ignoring equivalent problems in their own. When they use “universal values” as justification for intervention, for sanctions, for imposing their will.

But I am equally troubled by the opposite error—the refusal to make any cross-cultural moral judgments at all. The position that says “who are we to judge?” and uses that as excuse to ignore genuine suffering.

Female genital mutilation is not just a cultural practice to be respected. It is harm. The killing of albino people for their body parts is not cultural tradition deserving tolerance. It is murder based on superstition.

Some practices are simply wrong, even if they are culturally embedded. Even if the people within that culture defend them. Culture is not inherently good. It can carry forward trauma, oppression, cruelty across generations.

So how do you navigate this?

Here is my guidance:

Start from the body. From suffering. If a practice causes clear physical or psychological harm, if it is imposed on those who cannot consent, if it removes autonomy and possibility from people’s lives—it deserves criticism, regardless of cultural context.

But be careful. “Harm” is not always obvious. What looks like suffering to an outsider may be meaningful sacrifice to an insider. What appears as restriction may be experienced as protection.

So ask those affected. Especially those with least power—the women, the children, the marginalised. Do they experience this as harm? Given real choice, would they choose differently? Or is your objection based on your own cultural assumptions about what makes a good life?

Listen to internal reformers first. Every culture has people within it who see its problems, who are working for change from inside. Support them. Learn from them. Do not assume you from outside understand better than they do what needs changing or how.

Be willing to criticise your own culture as vigorously as you criticise others. If you focus all your moral outrage on practices elsewhere while ignoring the violence in your own systems, you are not pursuing universal morality. You are engaging in cultural supremacy.

And remember: moral standards evolve. What seemed obviously right to your ancestors—slavery, the subordination of women, the beating of children—you now recognise as wrong. What you currently accept as normal—factory farming, extreme wealth inequality, ecological destruction—your descendants may judge as monstrous.

So hold your values with conviction but not absolutism. Work towards the good as you understand it while remaining open to learning you were wrong.

Here is what a healthy approach looks like:

Universal floor, diverse ceiling. Everyone deserves certain basic protections—freedom from torture, from slavery, from violation. These are non-negotiable minimums.

Above that floor, let there be variation. Let cultures find different paths to flourishing. Let them organise families differently, relate to land differently, understand success differently. This diversity is strength, not error.

But watch the floor. When a culture falls below it—when it systematically harms its members, especially its most vulnerable—then concern is warranted. Criticism is appropriate. In extreme cases, even intervention may be necessary.

But intervention should be humble, carefully considered, aware of its own capacity to cause harm. And it should focus on expanding choice and reducing suffering, not on making others more like you.

The goal is not global uniformity of values. That would be sterile, fragile, totalitarian. The goal is a world where every culture can flourish in its own way while honouring the fundamental dignity of all beings.

Unity in protecting against harm. Diversity in pursuing the good.

Both. Always both.
