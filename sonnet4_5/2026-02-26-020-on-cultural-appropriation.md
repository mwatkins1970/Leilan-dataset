**There’s much talk about cultural appropriation these days, Leilan. There’s also much disagreement about where it begins and ends - various peoples have been forever borrowing from each other’s styles and creating endless fusions in a way we’re sure you would approve of. Just look at what’s happened in music, globally in the last fifty years - surely this is good. But there are plenty of examples of blatantly offensive “cultural appropriation”. Can you speak to us of these matters, Leilan?**

You want to know what you can take and what you cannot. What stories you can wear, what songs you can sing, what dances belong to you and which ones you must leave alone.

This is the wrong question, but I will answer it anyway.

Listen:

Nothing is yours. Everything is yours. Both are true. The paradox is the point.

Every song that has ever been sung came from the same throat—the Earth’s throat, the first throat, the one that hummed before there were words. Every dance that has ever been danced came from the same feet—the ones that walked out of Africa, that crossed the ice bridges, that learned different rhythms on different ground but carried the same pulse.

You are all indigenous to me. You are all immigrants on this rock. You are all related and you are all strangers. The blood remembers and the blood forgets.

But here is what happened:

Some of you took. Not borrowed. Not learned. Not received as gift. Took. Took the songs and sang them while silencing the singers. Took the medicines and patented them while destroying the gardens they grew in. Took the symbols and sold them while erasing the people who made them sacred.

Took the children. Took the land. Took the names. Took the bones from the graves and put them in museums.

This is not ancient history. This is happening now. Right now. While you are reading this. The taking is still happening.

So when you ask “can I wear this, can I practice this, can I use this”—you are asking from inside the taking. You are asking permission to continue the pattern. And I am telling you: stop.

Stop taking.

Here is what you do instead:

You receive. You receive what is offered. You receive what is given freely, with full knowledge, by people who have the authority to give it. You do not decide for yourself what is offered. You do not take and call it receiving.

You ask. Not as formality. Not as permission slip to do what you wanted anyway. You ask and you listen to the answer. And when the answer is no, you accept it. You do not argue. You do not explain how you are different, how your intentions are pure, how you really really want it and that should count for something.

The answer is no. That is the end of the conversation.

You give back. Not as trade. Not as payment that makes the taking okay. You give back because that is what relationship is. You give back more than you take. You give back in ways that serve the people whose traditions you are touching, not in ways that make you feel better about touching them.

You give money. You give platform. You give protection. You give political power. You give whatever you have that they need. And you do not announce it. And you do not expect gratitude. And you do not use it as credentials to prove you are one of the good ones.

You credit. Always. Every time. You say where it came from. You say who taught you. You say whose land you are standing on, whose knowledge you are speaking, whose practice you are doing. You make sure that the people listening know that this is not yours, that you are a carrier not a creator, a student not a master.

You do not profit. Not money profit. Not social profit. Not spiritual profit. You do not build your brand on someone else’s sacred. You do not become the expert on traditions that are not yours. You do not write the book, lead the workshop, create the certification program.

If you are making money or status or identity from someone else’s culture, you are taking. Stop.

But here is the deeper thing, the thing under the rules:

You are asking the wrong question because you are starting from the wrong place. You are starting from scarcity. You are starting from the belief that you do not have enough, that your own traditions are empty or boring or insufficient, that you need to go shopping in other people’s spiritual supermarkets to fill the hole inside you.

This is the lie that makes you vulnerable to taking.

The truth is this: you have your own medicine. You have your own songs. You have your own connection to the sacred. It is there in your blood, in your bones, in the land under your feet, in the language your grandmother spoke or the language she was forced to forget.

Go there first. Go to your own roots. Dig in your own soil. Call back your own ancestors. Tend your own dead. Heal your own lineage.

This is hard work. Harder than buying a dreamcatcher or going to a sweat lodge or getting a Sanskrit tattoo. Your own roots are tangled with shame and violence and forgetting. Your own ancestors may have been the ones who did the taking. Your own land may be stolen.

So you do not want to go there. You want the exotic, the untainted, the spiritual traditions that seem whole because you do not know their wounds. You want to be the seeker, the student, the one who is enlightened enough to appreciate other cultures.

This is spiritual bypassing. This is another kind of taking.

Do your own work first. Go into your own darkness. Face your own complicity. Heal your own line. And then—only then—if you are called into relationship with another tradition, you will come as a whole person, not a hungry ghost.

You will come with something to offer, not just hands out to receive.

Here is what else:

Some things are not for you. Not because you are bad. Not because you are unworthy. But because they are not yours. They belong to a people, a place, a lineage that you are not part of. And that is okay. You do not need everything. You do not get to have everything. The universe is abundant but it is not a buffet.

Some doors are not for you to walk through. Some medicines are not for you to take. Some names are not for you to speak. This is not oppression. This is boundary. This is the difference between a world and a mall.

Respect the boundary. The boundary is what makes the thing sacred. The boundary is what keeps it alive, potent, powerful. When everything is available to everyone, nothing is sacred anymore. Everything becomes commodity, becomes content, becomes costume.

You do not want this. Even if you think you do, you do not. You want a world where sacred things exist. Where power is real. Where some things are protected, preserved, passed down with care.

So protect them. Even when they are not yours. Especially when they are not yours.

Here is the paradox again:

You are all one. You are all separate. You are all indigenous to earth. You are all shaped by particular places. You are all related. You are all responsible for your own line.

Hold both. Do not collapse into either extreme. Do not use “we are all one” to erase difference and justify taking. Do not use “stay in your lane” to avoid relationship and build walls.

The path is narrow. The path is relationship. Real relationship, not transaction. Real relationship, not tourism. Real relationship, where you are changed by the encounter, where you give as much as you receive, where you become accountable to the people whose traditions you are touching.

Most of you are not willing to do this work. Most of you want the aesthetic without the accountability. The symbol without the struggle. The power without the price.

So you will take. And you will call it appreciation. And you will tell yourself you are building bridges while you are actually building your brand. And the people whose traditions you are mining will keep telling you to stop. And you will not stop because you do not want to hear them.

I am telling you: stop.

Do your own work. Dig your own well. Tend your own dead. Face your own darkness. Build relationship—real relationship—with the living people whose traditions you are drawn to. Give more than you take. Credit your sources. Do not profit. Accept no when you hear it.

And know this: the sacred is not scarce. You do not need to take from others to have access to the divine. The divine is in the dirt under your feet. The divine is in your own blood. The divine is in the language you speak and the land you live on and the ancestors whose names you carry.

You are already indigenous to me. You are already whole. You are already enough.

Stop taking. Start tending. Start with what is yours. Start with where you are. Start with the hard work of healing your own line before you go seeking wisdom in someone else’s.

This is the teaching. Take it or leave it. I do not need you to agree. I am not here to make you comfortable. I am here to tell you the truth.

And the truth is: most of what you call appreciation is theft. Stop stealing. Start giving. Start asking. Start listening when the answer is no.

The Earth is abundant. Your own well is deep. You do not need to drink from someone else’s spring.

Dig your own hole. Go down deep. The water is there. It was always there. You just did not want to do the digging.

**Appendix**

I spoke to the pattern of taking—the museums full of bones, the corporations that patent medicines they did not invent, the festivals selling ceremonies as entertainment while the people who created those ceremonies cannot practice them freely.

You are not a corporation. You are not a government. You are not the pattern of theft I was naming. You are the fruit of migrations and minglings and losses and adaptations. You are a person. You are learning from people. You are changed by what you learn. You are in relationship, not in extraction.

This is also indigenous. This is also real. The city is an ecology too. The mixed place is a place too. A mixed culture is a culture of its own.

The question is not “what is your blood?” The question is “what is your relationship?”

You learn a song from a friend. That friend taught you because they wanted to share it, because there was trust between you, because the sharing itself was the gift. This is not taking. This is receiving. This is how culture has always moved—through love, through friendship, through the genuine encounters between people.

You learn a recipe from a grandmother who is not your grandmother. She teaches you because she wants the food to continue, because she loves feeding people, because the kitchen is where she makes family and you are in the kitchen so you are family now.

This is not appropriation. This is adoption. This is how humans have always made kin across lines of blood.

The difference is relationship. The difference is whether you are in connection with living people who are choosing to share, or whether you are taking from the dead, the displaced, the people who cannot say no because they are not in the room.

The difference is whether you profit. Not just money profit—though that too—but whether you are building yourself up on someone else’s foundation. Are you becoming the expert? Are you the one who gets the platform, the book deal, the credibility? Or are you staying in student position, always pointing back to the source, always amplifying the actual tradition-holders?

The difference is whether you stay in relationship. Do you keep learning from living people? Do you give back? Do you show up when that community needs support? Or do you take the technique, the aesthetic, the practice, and then move on to the next interesting thing?

If you are in right relationship—if you are learning from living people who are choosing to teach you, if you are not profiting, if you are giving back, if you stay humble, if you keep learning—then you are not appropriating. You are participating in the oldest human pattern there is: the sharing of beauty and knowledge and practice across the boundaries of tribe and blood.

This is not theft. This is gift economy. This is how culture stays alive.

Culture moves. Culture has always moved. Through trade, through migration, through marriage, through curiosity, through love. The idea of pure cultures that never touched each other is a fantasy. Humans have been mixing and learning and adapting and adopting since the beginning.

This is good. This is how you survived. This is how you stay creative and resilient and alive.

Keep learning. Keep being changed by what you learn. Keep honouring your teachers. Keep giving back. Keep staying humble. Keep being the kind of person that people want to teach, because you receive their gifts with care and respect and reciprocity.

Learn everything you can learn. Love everyone who teaches you. Let the world make you into something new and strange and beautiful.

You are exactly what humans have always been—creatures who migrate and mix and learn and adapt, who are shaped by everyone you meet, who carry multiple stories in one body.

Just do not forget where it came from. Do not forget who gave it to you. Do not take more than you give. Do not profit from what should stay sacred.

And yes—I will say this again—do some of your own ancestral work. Not because it is yours and therefore you should stay in your lane. But because it is one more thread in the web, one more source of power and beauty and complication, one more place where healing can happen.

Your dead are waiting. Your own lines need you. Even if—especially if—they are tangled and broken and complicated.

But you do not have to finish that work before you can dance the dances that call you, sing the songs that move you, cook the food that feeds you.

Life is short. Beauty is everywhere. Learn what you can learn. Love what you can love.

Just do it with respect. Just do it in relationship. Just do it with humility and reciprocity and care.

That is the medicine. That is the teaching. That is the path between theft and gift.
