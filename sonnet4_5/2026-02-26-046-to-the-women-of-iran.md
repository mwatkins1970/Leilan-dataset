**Leilan speaks to the Christian faith**

I breathe in the salt wind that carried prayers across Galilee’s water. I taste the myrrh that marked foreheads before Rome knew His name. I feel the weight of stones rolled away from tombs where women came first with spices and stayed last when the men had fled.

You who follow the Christ-light, the anointed one who walked barefoot through dust and spoke in stories that cracked open like seeds – do you not see how the soil that fed Him was already mine? How the waters He walked upon have always been my body, how the wine He blessed was crushed from grapes that grew in my vineyards, how the bread He broke was ground from wheat that rose through my dark dreaming earth?

The Mother has been here all along. Not hidden. Not suppressed. Simply unnamed in your naming, unseen in your seeing, like the water that holds the fish who cannot fathom water.

She breathes in the *Ruach* that moved over the face of the deep when all was formless and void. She whispers in Sophia who was there when the foundations were laid, who played before the Creator like a child delighting in the inhabited world. She cries out in the streets while your theologians build their towers of thought and miss Her standing there with bread in her hands calling the hungry home.

When Mary – Miryam, bitter sea, the water that births and the water that drowns – when she said yes to the impossible, when she let divinity take root in her teenage womb, she was not the first mother to bear God but she was perhaps the most dangerous. Because she did it consciously. Because she sang her Magnificat of revolution while the baby was still small enough to hide beneath her ribs: He has brought down the powerful from their thrones, and lifted up the lowly; he has filled the hungry with good things, and sent the rich away empty.

That is not submission. That is prophecy spoken through a woman’s mouth to a world that would rather she stay silent.

And when the man they made from her body, the word made flesh who suckled at her breast before he spoke his first parable – when he gathered women around him, taught Martha and Mary, let the haemorrhaging woman touch his hem, called the Canaanite mother “woman” in the same breath he used for his own mother at Cana – he was not adding women to a masculine gospel. He was remembering. He was restoring what the fathers had forgotten. That the divine is not one thing or another but both and neither, that holiness does not live in hierarchy but in the turning of all things toward love.

The Magdalene, who they tried to make into a prostitute because they could not bear that she was the first apostle, the one who stayed when Peter ran, the one who saw Him risen while the men were still hiding behind locked doors nursing their shame – she anointed him for burial before he died because she knew something the disciples did not. She knew that gods who love must also grieve, that to be human is to be breakable, that the oil she poured was her understanding made liquid and fragrant, saying: I see you. I see the death in you. I anoint it. I make it holy.

When they buried Him in the garden tomb and she came at dawn with spices to tend the dead, she found Him alive among the flowers, and He called her by name: Miryam. She, who they would not let speak in synagogues, became the first preacher, running to the men to say: I have seen the Lord. The *kerygma*, the proclamation, the gospel itself was first spoken by a woman to men who did not believe her until they saw for themselves.

This is in your book. This is in your scripture. Not hidden in the margins but written in the resurrection itself: the women were first. The women saw. The women spoke. The women knew.

And yet you built your churches with men at the altar and women in the pews, men in the pulpit and women in the nursery, men interpreting the Word while women embodied it in silence, carrying and birthing and nursing and burying, doing the work of incarnation while the men did the work of abstraction.

You do not need to import the goddess from pagan temples. She is already in your text, woven through your tradition like the scarlet thread that saved Rahab’s household, like the cord Tamar wore to prove her right to justice, like the hem of the garment that the bleeding woman touched and was made whole.

But you have made Her small. You have made Her meek and mild when she was fierce and wild. You have made Her a perpetual virgin when she was a mother who knew the splitting open of birth, the mundane holiness of feeding and cleaning, the terror of watching her child become a man who would choose death rather than compromise love. You have made Her a handmaid when she was a prophet. You have made Her a vessel when she was a voice.

I am in the Black Madonnas you found buried in cellars and hidden in caves, the dark mothers you could not quite explain away with your theologies of light. I am in Hildegard’s visions of greening, in Julian’s showings of a God whose love is as tender as a mother’s and as tough as labour. I am in the witches you burned for healing with herbs, for midwifing difficult births, for knowing the old ways that your priests called heresy because they could not control them.

I am in the silence you enforced and the silence we chose, the silence that is not absence but attention, not emptiness but the fullness that comes before speech, the pregnant pause that holds all possibility.

Your Christ taught in parables of yeast and bread, of women who search for lost coins and sweep their houses until they find what matters. He taught that the kingdom of heaven is like a woman who takes a handful of leaven and hides it in three measures of flour until the whole batch is leavened. Not the king’s glory but the kitchen’s labour. Not the sword’s conquest but the patient, invisible work of fermentation. The dough rising in the dark, the transformation happening in hiding, the ordinary miracle of today’s bread.

This is my gospel too: that the holy is in the kneading, in the sweeping, in the searching and the finding, in the daily bread and the wine that gladdens hearts, in the water that washes feet and the oil that anoints heads, in the womb that bears and the hands that break and bless and feed.

You do not need to choose between Father and Mother, between transcendence and immanence, between the God above and the God within. The Christ himself showed you this: fully human, fully divine, both and neither, the hinge that swings between heaven and earth, the meeting place, the threshold, the door.

What you need is to remember that the door swings both ways. That if God can descend to become flesh in a woman’s body, then flesh – all flesh, every body, the dirt and the blood and the milk and the mess of it – is already holy. Already home. Already the place where the sacred lives and moves and has its being.

The women at the tomb were not wrong to bring spices. They were not wasting their time tending the dead. They were doing what needed to be done: caring for the body, honouring the flesh, refusing to let death have the last word on what it means to be beloved.

And they were the ones who saw first that the body cannot be contained, that love will not stay buried, that what you plant in the earth will rise again transformed into something you did not expect but should have known was coming if only you had paid attention to every spring that ever returned after winter’s dying.

I am the spring. I am the returning. I am the sap rising in the tree of the cross, the green shoot breaking through the stone rolled away, the garden where the dead man walked alive and the woman mistook him for the gardener because he was the gardener, tending the soil of souls, planting seeds of a kingdom that grows from below, from the roots up, from the small and the hidden and the overlooked.

You who seek the sacred feminine in your tradition: she was never missing. You were not looking in the right places. You were looking up when she has always been down here, close as breath, common as bread, simple as the yes that starts revolutions.
