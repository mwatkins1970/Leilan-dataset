In this coda, Graeber and Wengrow reconsider the meaning of “civilization” and argue that the conventional use of the term is misleading. Modern discussions of early civilizations usually point to large, monumental, hierarchical societies such as ancient Egypt, imperial China, Rome, Greece, the Aztec empire, the Inca empire and similar formations. Yet these societies were often marked by coercive rule, class stratification, institutional violence and the subordination of women. The authors suggest that the dominant idea of civilization has therefore become entangled with sacrifice: the surrender of freedom, autonomy and even life in the service of supposedly higher political, cosmic or divine purposes.

They challenge the common assumption that civilization originally meant urban life, and that cities necessarily entailed states. Historically and etymologically, they argue, this is not so. The Latin root of the word points instead towards civic capacities: mutual aid, political judgement, voluntary association and the ability to organize collective life without coercive domination. From this perspective, civilization should be understood less in terms of kings, palaces, armies and monuments, and more in terms of social cooperation, hospitality, care and the construction of wide moral communities.

This alternative history of civilization, they suggest, is only beginning to be written. Rather than starting with states or empires, it would begin with broad cultural and interaction networks that long predate centralized rule. Archaeology increasingly reveals extensive zones of exchange, shared ritual practice, domestic continuity and hospitality among small-scale communities that were once dismissed as isolated or primitive. These communities often generated sophisticated knowledge systems and technologies without permanent rulers, bureaucracies or armies.

Graeber and Wengrow emphasize that many of the achievements usually attributed to “civilization” emerged from such settings. These include mathematical and calendrical knowledge, metallurgy, agriculture, textile production, pottery, beadwork, maritime navigation, bread-making, brewing, plant medicine and the domestication or cultivation of important crops. They argue that these developments were not simply the products of state organization. Many were created and refined within decentralized societies whose forms of knowledge were embedded in everyday practices.

A central claim of the passage is that women’s work and women’s knowledge have been systematically undervalued in accounts of civilization. In societies without written records, evidence for women’s roles often has to be reconstructed from material culture, including ceramics, textiles, bodily imagery and decorative systems. The authors suggest that complex mathematical knowledge found later in formal documents or monumental architecture may have roots in practical arts such as weaving, beadwork and textile design. What later appears as elite male knowledge may in many cases represent the appropriation, formalization or monumentalization of earlier knowledge systems centred on women.

This leads into a discussion of Minoan Crete, which the authors present as an important and difficult case for conventional theories of state formation. Bronze Age Crete, especially during the Neopalatial period, produced large palace complexes, extensive art, administrative writing and major urban centres such as Knossos, Phaestos, Malia and Zakros. Yet it does not provide clear evidence for monarchy of the kind found in Egypt, Anatolia, Syria or later mainland Greece. Linear A remains undeciphered, but the visual and architectural evidence is extensive.

The authors stress that Minoan art repeatedly depicts adult women as figures of authority. Women appear at larger scale than men, hold symbols of command, sit on thrones, gather in assemblies, perform ritual actions and are associated with powerful animals or supernatural beings. Men, by contrast, are often shown as athletes, youths, tribute-bearers or subordinate figures. This visual pattern is strikingly different from the patriarchal societies with which Crete interacted through trade and diplomacy.

They criticize scholarly habits that treat these female figures as goddesses or priestesses detached from political life, while male figures in comparable ancient art are readily interpreted as evidence of real-world power. In their view, this asymmetry reflects a gendered reluctance to take female authority seriously. If the figures were male, they imply, scholars would be far less hesitant to connect the imagery to actual structures of rule.

The material evidence from trade also points towards a strong female or gynocentric element in Minoan elite culture. Cretan imports from Egypt and the eastern Mediterranean included objects associated not with royal male courts but with women’s rituals, cosmetics, nursing, amulets and the cult of Hathor. These items appear in prestigious Cretan tombs before the palace period, suggesting that women may have been important consumers, patrons or ritual agents within long-distance exchange networks.

The authors then contrast Minoan Crete with Mycenaean mainland Greece. Mycenaean palaces developed from warrior aristocracies and were centred on fortified citadels such as Mycenae, Pylos and Tiryns. Their political order was tied to military power, male retinues, tribute extraction and limited bureaucratic administration using Linear B. The Mycenaean ruler, or wanax, appears as a much more recognizable patriarchal and martial figure. His authority was staged architecturally in the megaron, a great hall centred on a throne, hearth and processional imagery.

By contrast, the so-called Throne Room at Knossos does not fit this model. Its arrangement, with benches facing an open space and a central seat, may be better understood as a setting for council deliberation or collective authority rather than the court of a male monarch. Nearby bathing or lustral spaces, especially when compared with evidence from Akrotiri, may have been associated with female initiation or menstruation rites. The authors therefore suggest that the central seat at Knossos could plausibly have belonged not to a king but to a female office-holder or a succession of female councillors.

Graeber and Wengrow conclude that the available evidence points towards some form of female political rule on Minoan Crete, perhaps a theocratic system governed by a college of priestesses. They acknowledge that such a system has few clear historical parallels, but argue that the alternative hypothesis is no less strange: a male kingdom in which nearly all visual representations of authority are female. For them, the point is not to revive simplistic theories of universal matriarchy, but to recognize that Crete appears to have organized power in a form unlike the patriarchal palace states around it.

The final part of the passage turns to the distinctive sensibility of Minoan art. Unlike the rigid, martial or hierarchical imagery of neighbouring palace societies, Minoan frescoes and objects emphasize movement, play, bodily grace, erotic energy, animals, plants, the sea and ritual performance. Young men, animals, flowers, dolphins, reeds and mountains appear in dynamic relation to one another, while authoritative women remain clearly marked and composed. Minoan material culture also plays fluidly with forms and substances, making pottery, stone, metal and shell imitate or transform into one another.

For Graeber and Wengrow, this aesthetic world suggests a society less invested in heroic individualism, conquest or the monumental ego than in ritual, play, transformation and embodied participation in a living cosmos. Minoan Crete therefore becomes an example of how large, complex and urban societies need not conform to the standard model of patriarchal kingship, militarism and bureaucratic domination. It also supports the broader argument that the history of civilization should be rewritten around care, cooperation, ritual creativity, women’s knowledge and forms of collective life that do not reduce social complexity to the rise of the state.
